When people ask me whether it makes sense to **invest in Georgia**, they usually talk about yields, price per square metre or sea views. Rarely about the state budget. Yet when you buy an apartment in a country, you also buy its trajectory: its debt, its currency, its roads, its ability to hold up over time.

On 30 September 2026, the Ministry of Finance submitted the draft 2027 budget to Parliament. I read it as an entrepreneur, the way I would read a company's accounts before putting money into it. Here is what I take from it.

## Debt at 31.6% of GDP: the figure that matters

The draft sets public debt at **31.6% of GDP** and caps the deficit at **2.5% of GDP**, according to ministry figures reported by [Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/georgias-2027-consolidated-budget-set-at-gel-37-5-billion-finance-ministry-says/) on 1 October. The consolidated budget reaches 37.5 billion lari, 1.5 billion more than the 2026 forecast. Tax revenue is expected to rise by 2.1 billion to 29.5 billion lari, **with no tax increase announced**.

For comparison, French public debt is above 110% of GDP. I am not saying Georgia is Switzerland. I am saying that a state with little debt has fewer reasons to reach into the pockets of property owners and businesses. That is exactly what is happening in France, where every budget trims one more tax advantage.

For a property investor, this is a point of stability. A tax system that does not change every year lets you calculate a ten-year return without nasty surprises.

## 8.4 billion lari for infrastructure

The figure that interests me most is public investment. The draft plans more than **8.4 billion lari of capital expenditure**, almost one billion more than in 2026. In detail, again according to the ministry:

- more than 2.7 billion for municipal infrastructure;
- up to 1.3 billion for roads, including 723 million for motorways;
- 432 million for water and sanitation, 404 million for energy;
- 250 million to increase rail network capacity;
- 125 million for the Anaklia deep-water port;
- 125 million to modernise the Tbilisi metro;
- 60 million to complete the runway at Kutaisi International Airport.

The rail budget pays for 45 locomotives and 10 passenger trains, co-financed by the World Bank, the ADB and the AIIB, according to [Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/government-plans-to-take-%E2%82%BE2-billion-in-new-external-debt-in-2027/). The list of external lenders for 2027 speaks for itself: ADB, World Bank, EIB, KfW, EBRD, AFD. The major lenders keep financing the country.

## What this investment means for real estate in Georgia

I do not believe in promises like "the metro is coming, prices will double". I do believe in a simple rule: **real estate follows infrastructure, a few years behind**.

In Tbilisi, the metro upgrade adds value to well-served neighbourhoods. When I look at a project, the first thing I check is the walking distance to a station. That is what makes the difference for rentals, far more than a rooftop pool.

In the west, Anaklia, Kutaisi and the new roads tell another story. A deep-water port, a growing airport and motorways that shorten journeys bring Batumi, Kutaisi and the coast closer to the rest of the country. For short-term rentals, a longer season and guests who arrive more easily mean extra revenue.

I stay cautious about timelines. Anaklia is a project that has been relaunched several times. I factor it into a long-term view, never into a two-year return calculation.

## 5% growth: the assumptions, and what to watch

The budget assumes real growth of **5% in 2027**, then 5.2 to 5.3% in the following years. The ministry targets GDP per capita above 12,000 dollars by the end of 2027 and 15,000 dollars by 2030, with inflation back at the National Bank's 3% target by the end of 2027.

These are assumptions, not guarantees. Three points deserve attention:

- **Inflation.** It stood at 5.9% year on year in October 2026, according to Geostat. It has to come down for the National Bank to cut rates, which would make mortgages more affordable.
- **The final vote.** Parliament is due to adopt the text in December. The amounts can still move.
- **The regional environment.** 5% growth assumes that the transit corridor and tourism hold up. They do today, but nothing is guaranteed.

What I like is that the assumptions remain cautious. The National Bank expects stronger growth for 2026. A budget built on 5% therefore has some headroom.

## My reading as an investor

A country with little debt, investing in its roads, its railways, its metro and its port, and leaving its tax system alone: that is the framework I look for in a long-term investment. It is not a reason to buy just anything. The choice of developer, location and type of property remains decisive.
