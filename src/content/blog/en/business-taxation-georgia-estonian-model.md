For more than ten years at the head of Swiss Auto Glass, I thought like every Swiss business owner. At year-end, profit is taxed, whether you keep it in the company or pay it out. The tax falls on the year's result, not on the decision you make afterwards. In Georgia, the logic is reversed. **Business taxation in Georgia** rests on what is called the "Estonian model": the company pays nothing as long as it keeps its profit and reinvests it.

On paper, it is simple. In practice, this model changes the way you run a company. It also has traps I regularly see among entrepreneurs who set up here.

## What the rule says

Since 1 January 2017, Georgian profit tax no longer hits the accounting result. It hits the flows that leave the company. According to [PwC](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/Georgia/Corporate/Taxes-on-corporate-income), whose summary was revised in September 2026, the rate is 15%. It applies to four categories:

- **distributed profits**, in cash or in kind;
- **expenses unrelated to the business**, including unjustified ones;
- **free-of-charge benefits**: goods, services or funds supplied with nothing in return, and even inventory shortages;
- **representation expenses**, for the part above 1% of the previous year's income or expenses.

Banks, microfinance institutions and lenders have been taxed at 20% since 2023. For a typical SME, the rule remains 15%.

One detail matters: the rate applies to the "gross" amount. As [Andersen Georgia](https://ge.andersen.com/georgian-corporate-income-tax-regime/) explains, the distributed amount is divided by 0.85 before applying the 15%. To pay out 85,000 lari in dividends, the company pays 15,000 lari in tax. Measured against the amount paid out, that is about 17.65%. You need to know this before building a cash plan.

## What it changes for running an SME

Take a company with a profit of 200,000 francs. In Switzerland, the average corporate profit tax rate is 14.43% in 2026, according to [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/ch/en/media/press-releases/2026/05/clarity-swiss-taxes.html). It ranges from 11.66% in Lucerne to 20.54% in Bern. At the average rate, this company pays around 29,000 francs in tax, even if it reinvests every franc in machines, a new site or new hires.

In Georgia, if it reinvests everything, it pays nothing that year. The tax only arrives the day the money leaves.

In concrete terms, I see three effects on management.

**Growth is easier to finance from equity.** Every lari kept works at 100%. For a company in its development phase, that is cash available without borrowing. Every Swiss business owner knows the moment when you have to choose between investing and setting aside the year's tax. Here, that trade-off disappears.

**The dividend becomes a real strategic decision.** You no longer distribute "what is left after tax". You decide when and how much to take out, knowing the exact cost. This forces shareholder and manager to talk openly: what does the company need over the next two years?

**Discipline on spending becomes vital.** A poorly justified expense is not just disallowed as a deduction, as elsewhere. It is treated as a distribution and taxed immediately. Rigorous bookkeeping is no longer a formality.

## The traps I see most often

The model is generous, but it does not forgive approximation. Here is what I regularly come across.

- **"Personal" expenses run through the company.** The car used by the family, the trip that mixes business and holidays. In Georgia, that is a disguised distribution, taxed just like a dividend.
- **Loans to the shareholder.** According to PwC, a loan granted to an individual or to a non-resident is one of the taxable flows. This common reflex to "take out" cash without a dividend does not work here.
- **Representation expenses.** The 1% cap is quickly reached in a small structure that entertains a lot.
- **The filing rhythm.** The tax period is the month. The return and payment are due on the 15th of the following month, according to Andersen. You need bookkeeping kept up to date continuously, not rebuilt at year-end.
- **Forgetting tax residence.** A Georgian company actually managed from Geneva or Zurich may be considered taxable in Switzerland, where its effective management is located. The Estonian model solves nothing if the substance is not in Georgia.

## And for the Swiss shareholder?

You have to look at the level above. According to [PwC](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/georgia/corporate/withholding-taxes), Georgian withholding tax on dividends paid to a non-resident company is 5% under domestic law. For a Swiss company holding at least 10% of the Georgian company, the treaty between the two countries reduces this rate to 0%. Below that threshold, it rises to 10%.

A structure with a Swiss holding company can therefore bring dividends up without Georgian withholding tax. Provided that the holding has a genuine reason to exist and that each level is documented. I always recommend having the structure validated by a tax adviser on both sides before signing anything.

## My advice before setting up a company in Georgia

The Estonian model is not a tax loophole. It is a management tool. It rewards entrepreneurs who reinvest, who keep clean accounts and who strictly separate their personal assets from the company's. It penalises those who treat the company as a current account.

Before you **set up a company in Georgia**, ask yourself three questions. What share of the profit will you reinvest over three years? Where will decisions really be made? Who will keep the books month after month?
