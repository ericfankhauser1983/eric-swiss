Every year it is the same. Around mid-December, my phone rings more often. Entrepreneurs who have set up a company in Georgia all ask me the same question: "How do I take the money out, and what will it cost me?" **Business taxation in Georgia** is simple on paper. But between the tax on distributed profit, the withholding on the dividend and Swiss taxation for those who still live there, the final bill depends on a few decisions that are better made before year-end than after.

Here is how I think it through with my clients, with numbers.

## Two layers of tax, not one

In Georgia, a company pays no tax on the profit it keeps. This is the "Estonian model", in force since 2017, which I described in [an earlier article](/en/blog/business-taxation-georgia-estonian-model/). The tax falls when the money leaves the company.

When you pay yourself a dividend, two charges follow one another.

- **At company level: 15%.** According to [PwC](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/Georgia/Corporate/Taxes-on-corporate-income) (reviewed in September 2026), the rate applies to the "grossed-up" value of the distribution. In practice, out of GEL 100,000 the company wants to release, 15,000 goes in tax and 85,000 is paid to the shareholder.
- **At shareholder level: 5%.** This is Georgian withholding tax on dividends, deducted by the company when it pays. According to [Andersen Georgia](https://ge.andersen.com/?p=7170), this standard 5% rate applies to residents and non-residents alike.

On our GEL 100,000, the shareholder therefore receives 85,000 minus 4,250, or **GEL 80,750 net**. The total burden is about 19.25%. That is low compared with Switzerland, but it is still almost a fifth, and it needs planning.

## Dividend or salary: the calculation I do with my clients

The second route is salary. If you genuinely work for your company in Georgia, it can pay you. Salary is an operating expense, so it does not trigger the 15%. It is, however, subject to 20% income tax withheld at source. On top of that, if you are enrolled in the Georgian pension scheme, there is a 2% employee contribution and a 2% employer contribution, again according to Andersen.

Take the same GEL 100,000 of total cost to the company.

- **Gross salary:** about GEL 98,040, once the 2% employer share is deducted.
- **Income tax (20%):** about GEL 19,610.
- **Employee contribution (2%):** about GEL 1,960.
- **Net in your pocket:** about **GEL 76,470**, plus close to GEL 3,920 paid into your pension account.

So the dividend leaves slightly more cash: about 80,750 against 76,470. The gap is not huge, and salary has other advantages: a regular income, proof of income for a bank or a landlord, retirement savings. With my clients, I often combine the two: a reasonable salary that matches the work actually done, and a dividend decided once a year, when the accounts are closed and the company's needs are clear.

One important point: a salary must match a real role. A fictitious salary paid to someone who does not work for the company will be requalified. And the third route, a loan to the shareholder, does not work in Georgia: PwC lists it among the transactions taxed as a distribution.

## If you still live in Switzerland: the third layer

Many of my clients keep their residence in Switzerland during the first years. In that case, the Georgian dividend is also taxable income in Switzerland.

The [Switzerland–Georgia tax treaty](https://www.mof.ge/files/download/Swiss%20Confederation.pdf/31ae9b95-ab5f-455a-814c-d32dbc095c2c) caps Georgian withholding at 10% for a Swiss resident. Because Georgia's domestic rate is only 5%, the 5% applies. Article 23 of the treaty then provides relief in Switzerland for the tax levied in Georgia on these dividends.

For a holding of at least 10% in the company, Switzerland also applies partial taxation of dividends: 70% of the dividend is taxable at federal level and at least 50% at cantonal level, as [Vontobel](https://www.vontobel.com/en-ch/impact/dividend-taxation-swiss-equities-in-switzerland-50912/) summarises. The final rate depends on your canton and your other income. This is where the difference between two entrepreneurs becomes significant.

For a Swiss company that directly holds at least 10% of the Georgian company, the treaty reduces withholding to 0%. That is the holding logic, to be handled with care: the holding must have real substance.

## My checklist before the 31 December close

Here is what I review with every client in December.

1. **The company's needs over twelve months.** Investments, hiring, a cash buffer. What funds growth stays in the company, tax-free.
2. **The amount to take out, and why.** A dividend is decided for a specific project: a property purchase, a contribution, savings. Not because "there is money left".
3. **The right salary–dividend mix**, depending on your residence and your actual role.
4. **The year's expenses.** Any expense unrelated to the business is taxed at 15% like a dividend. I have the ledger reviewed before the close, not after.
5. **The timing.** In Georgia the tax period is the month: distribution tax and withholding are declared and paid by the 15th of the following month. A dividend paid at the end of December is therefore settled in mid-January, and the cash must be there.
6. **The paperwork.** Shareholder resolution, minutes, proof of tax residence if you rely on the treaty.

## My advice

In Georgia, taking money out of your company costs less than in Switzerland. But the tax advantage only pays off if the decision is prepared: how much, when, in what form and from which country of residence. This is exactly the kind of trade-off that takes an hour with the right numbers, and costs a lot when improvised on 30 December.
