On 30 October, Fitch issued two decisions on the same day. It affirmed Georgia's rating at BB and raised its outlook from "stable" to "positive". It affirmed France at A+, but cut its outlook from "stable" to "negative". Two countries, two opposite trajectories. For anyone looking to **invest in Georgia**, this coincidence says something important about the direction of travel.

## What Fitch decided for Georgia

A positive outlook means an upgrade is possible within 12 to 24 months. According to the Georgian Ministry of Finance, Fitch bases its decision on three factors: the rapid accumulation of international reserves, a low current account deficit and strong growth ([Sova News](https://sovanews.tv/en/2026/10/31/fitch-revises-georgias-rating-outlook-to-positive-affirms-at-bb/)). The agency also praises prudent fiscal policy, moderate public debt and a stable banking system.

The figures behind the decision, as reported by FFI:

- **Foreign exchange reserves:** $8.2 billion in September 2026, a record, about $2 billion more than at the end of 2025 ([Trend](https://www.trend.az/scaucasus/georgia/4241382.html)).
- **Current account deficit:** about 1.5% of GDP in 2026, against 2.9% in 2025 and 5.1% in 2024.
- **Growth:** Fitch forecasts 7.5% in 2026. Over the first nine months, the average measured by Geostat is 7.4%.
- **Public debt:** the 2027 draft budget puts it at 31.6% of GDP, as I explained in [my article on the 2027 budget](/en/blog/budget-2027-georgia-debt-investment-investing/).

S&P had already taken the same decision on 7 August 2026, according to FFI. Two of the three agencies are saying the same thing.

## The same day, France moves to a negative outlook

For France, the picture is the reverse. Fitch cites weaker growth, higher borrowing costs and a wider deficit than in August. The agency writes that the budget debate "has not materially strengthened" its confidence in durable deficit reduction ([Reuters via WSAU](https://wsau.com/2026/10/30/fitch-revises-frances-outlook-to-negative-on-worsening-debt-dynamics/)).

The figures: public debt expected to rise from 115.7% of GDP in 2025 to around 124% in 2028, GDP down 0.1% in the third quarter, and a 10-year yield at its highest since 2002, above 5.14%. A few days earlier, Moody's had also kept France on a negative outlook ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/31/france-aims-for-responsible-2027-budget-as-fitch-downgrades-outlook)).

I am not comparing an A+ and a BB as if they were equal. France remains far better rated. But a rating is a snapshot; the outlook is the direction. And the two directions are opposite.

## What the rating concretely changes for an investor

A sovereign rating is not a quality label for real estate. Yet it has very concrete effects:

- **The cost of credit.** A better rating lowers what the state and banks pay to borrow abroad. Over time, mortgages in Georgia may become cheaper.
- **The currency.** High reserves give the National Bank the means to defend the lari. For an investor receiving rent in lari, that is a protection.
- **Foreign capital.** Many funds only invest in countries above a certain rating threshold. Each notch gained widens the pool of potential buyers, for companies and for real estate alike.

The next step would be BB+. The "investment grade" category, which opens the door to large institutional funds, starts at BBB-. We are still two notches away.

## The risks Fitch does not hide

I do not sell dreams, and neither does Fitch. The agency cites several weaknesses, which FFI reports:

1. **High dollarisation** of the economy, and part of public debt in foreign currency.
2. **High net external debt.**
3. **The durability of exports**, partly driven by vehicle re-exports and the refining of imported oil.

Fitch also expects the current account deficit to widen to around 3.5% of GDP in 2027 and 2028. These are the points I watch, not the press releases.

## What I take from it

Georgia remains an emerging market, with the risks that come with it. But the trajectory is measurable, and it is confirmed by two rating agencies: rising reserves, low debt, strong growth. Meanwhile, France sees its debt climbing and its rates rising.

For an investor, I am not saying you must choose one against the other. I am saying it is healthy to diversify towards a country moving in the right direction.
