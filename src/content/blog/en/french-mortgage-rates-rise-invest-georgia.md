In one month, **French mortgage rates** have risen by 10 to 25 basis points. Over 20 years, the average is around 3.7%, and several banks are already lending above 4%. This is not a disaster. But for an investor it is a clear signal: leverage, which made French buy-to-let landlords rich for fifteen years, works less and less. Let me explain why, and how I think about how to **invest in Georgia** without depending on credit.

## What happened in October and November

Broker figures converge. In early November, Meilleurtaux shows 3.50% over 15 years, 3.70% over 20 years and 3.80% over 25 years, against roughly 3.40%, 3.50% and 3.60% in October ([Mediaterranee](https://www.mediaterranee.com/1022026-taux-immobilier-novembre-2026-370-sur-20-ans-selon-meilleurtaux.html)). Pretto gives 3.56%, 3.75% and 3.93%. Maxime Chipoy of Meilleurtaux sums it up: the real increase is "about 25 points in a month" ([France Transactions](https://www.francetransactions.com/actus/immobilier/taux-immo/credit-immobilier-forte-hausse-taux-novembre.html)).

Banks moved fast. Crédit Mutuel du Sud-Ouest raised its rates by 0.25 points on 19 October, Crédit Agricole Île-de-France by 0.20 points on 21 October ([ABC Bourse](https://www.abcbourse.com/marches/credit-immobilier-voici-les-taux-sur-15-20-et-25-ans-en-novembre-2026_705489)). At Vousfinancer, Sandrine Allonier notes that "some banks now lend at more than 4% over 20 years" ([Bourse Inside](https://bourseinside.fr/impots/credit-immobilier-le-seuil-de-4-sur-20-ans-est-franchi-voici-le-surcout-de-plusieurs-milliers-deuros-pour-votre-projet/)).

And it is not equal for everyone. According to Pretto, a borrower earning less than €40,000 a year pays about 4.07% over 25 years. Above €120,000, it is 3.53%.

## Why rates are rising: the French state is borrowing at a higher cost

The main cause is not your local bank. It is public debt. On 21 October, the 10-year OAT yield crossed 5% for the first time since 2002, peaking at 5.13% the following day. In early September it was around 4.1%. On top of that, the ECB has raised rates by half a point since June, according to Pretto.

The political context does not help. On 4 November, the National Assembly rejected the revenue part of the 2027 budget by 458 votes to none. The 2027 deficit is now estimated at 5.9% of GDP, instead of the 5% target ([Économie Matin](https://www.economiematin.fr/budget-2027-rejet-unanime-deficit-france-impasse)). When a state pays more to borrow, its banks pass the increase on to households. It is mechanical.

The usury rate, which caps the total cost of a loan, is set at 5.40% for loans of 20 years or more until 31 December. Meilleurtaux observes that it is starting to block smaller applications.

## What it really costs

Take a concrete example given by brokers. For €250,000 over 20 years at 3.76%, the monthly payment is about €1,484 excluding insurance. The November rise alone adds €13 a month, or €3,115 over the life of the loan (Pretto).

On €220,000 borrowed over 22 years, going from 3.40% to 3.80% costs about €12,200 in extra interest. In Rennes, a budget of €1,000 a month over 25 years buys 5.75 m² less than a year ago (CAFPI).

Twelve thousand euros will not ruin anyone. But it is often the equivalent of one or two years of positive cash flow on a rental investment.

## Leverage: the rule every investor must check

I explained it in my article on [return on equity](/en/blog/return-on-equity-leverage-real-estate-georgia/). Credit only creates wealth if the property earns more than the borrowed money costs.

A simple, purely illustrative example. An apartment yielding 3.5% net of costs and taxes, financed at 3.8% plus insurance: every euro borrowed costs more than it earns. Leverage turns negative. You are left relying on capital growth alone. Yet French prices fell 0.1% in October, their first national monthly decline of the year, with 8 of 11 major cities down ([MySweetImmo](https://www.mysweetimmo.com/2026/11/02/prix-immobiliers-novembre-2026/)).

Put simply, with rates around 4%, many classic buy-to-let investments in France become bets on prices rather than yield investments.

## How I think about Georgia

Let's be honest: credit is not cheap in Georgia either. The National Bank raised its key rate to 8.25% in May 2026 ([JAMnews](https://jam-news.net/georgias-national-bank-raises-refinancing-rate-to-8-25-making-loans-more-expensive/)). Mortgages in dollars start at around 8%, those in lari exceed 12% ([GBC](https://www.gbc.ge/public/en/news/finanse/mortgage-issuances-to-non-residents-have-increased)). So I do not recommend buying in Georgia with local credit, except in specific cases.

The difference lies elsewhere:

- **The entry ticket.** At Georgian prices, you can buy a whole property with your own funds. No leverage needed to get started.
- **The developer's payment plan.** On new builds, many developers offer interest-free instalments until delivery. It is leverage, but without a bank and without a rate.
- **Taxation.** Rental income for individuals is taxed at 5%. Capital gains are exempt after two years of ownership.
- **Yield is calculated on the money actually invested**, not on a structure where the bank takes the margin.

I am not saying you should sell in France. I am saying that in 2027, an investor looking for yield can no longer think only in monthly payments. You have to look at the rate, the net yield and the taxation, side by side.
