The war in Iran is entering its ninth month and the Strait of Hormuz is still closed. G7 foreign ministers met in Paris. Meanwhile, in Islamabad, Tokyo, Seoul and New Delhi, people are asking a different question: not "who is right?" but "how do we get the tankers moving again?". From Tbilisi, I find that shift in tone more useful than European statements. It also says something concrete to anyone who wants to **invest in Georgia** or set up a business here.

## Where the strait stands

On 11 November, Iran's chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said Hormuz would "never return" to its pre-war status. He referred to an understanding with Oman on managing the passage ([Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/11/asias-way-out-of-the-hormuz-deadlock/)). At the same time, security chief Mohsen Rezaei called reports of talks with Washington a "pure lie". In his words, Tehran is still waiting for a clear answer to its seven conditions: lifting sanctions and the US naval blockade, and ending the fighting on all fronts, especially in Lebanon ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/iran-denies-talks-with-us-says-hormuz-will-stay-shut-until-demands-are-met)).

On the American side, Donald Trump calls the war "militarily effectively over" but keeps the blockade in place. In Paris, Marco Rubio said Washington was "open to diplomacy", adding that economic pressure made major new combat operations unnecessary.

On the water, things are less diplomatic. Maritime monitors counted at least 37 attacks on ships in and around the strait in October, the highest monthly total since the war began ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/iran-denies-talks-with-us-says-hormuz-will-stay-shut-until-demands-are-met)). On 5 November, after new tanker attacks, Brent rose by 3.67 dollars to 98.29 dollars a barrel.

## The real question: who pays, and for what

In Europe, the debate is mostly about principles: freedom of navigation, Iran's responsibility, international law. These are fair questions. But they are not the questions of the countries that depend most on the strait.

The numbers are simple. In 2025, nearly 15 million barrels a day went through Hormuz, and China and India alone took 44% of it, according to the IEA. Japan shipped 93% of its Middle Eastern crude through the strait ([Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/11/asias-way-out-of-the-hormuz-deadlock/)). For Tokyo, Seoul or New Delhi, Hormuz is not a foreign policy file. It is a supply issue.

Hence a very down-to-earth discussion about the price of passage. According to Reuters, quoted by Asia Times, Iran is asking for a transit fee of 5 to 7% of cargo value. Oman has reportedly mentioned around 3%. Washington rejects any toll. It is not glamorous, but it is a commercial negotiation, with numbers on the table.

## The proposal from Asia

In an op-ed published on 13 November, Bilal Habib Qazi, an analyst at the Institute of Regional Studies in Islamabad, suggests a way out that I find interesting for its logic. It is an opinion, not an official plan, but it captures how people think about the problem in Asia ([Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/11/asias-way-out-of-the-hormuz-deadlock/)).

The idea: **pay for the oil, not the passage.** Japan, South Korea and India would resume limited purchases of Iranian crude under a US waiver. The money would go into local-currency accounts in the buyers' banks. Iran could use it only to import from those countries. In return, the waiver would require free, toll-free and non-discriminatory passage, checked against public tanker-tracking data.

The author admits the limits himself. The plan does not settle the nuclear file, Lebanon or sanctions relief. And a similar mechanism has disappointed before: about 6 billion dollars of Iranian oil revenue, moved from South Korea to Qatar in 2023, was frozen again after 7 October.

The next meeting is not in Brussels or Paris. It is the APEC summit in Shenzhen on 18 and 19 November, under Chinese chairmanship ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/china-readies-shenzhen-apec-summit-nov-18/)). In May, Xi Jinping and Donald Trump agreed that Hormuz "must remain open", Asia Times recalls. Shenzhen is where Asia's big importers and Washington can talk oil.

## What it means, seen from Tbilisi

What strikes me is how absent Europe is from the search for a practical solution. Yet it is directly affected: through energy prices, freight and imported inflation. It comments more than it negotiates. The Gulf, Oman, Qatar, Pakistan and now East Asia are filling the space.

For the Caucasus, this crisis has had a very concrete effect: it has put land routes back at the centre. In the first week of March, demand on the Trans-Caspian route was up 450 to 500% on a year earlier, and turnaround times at the ports of Aktau and Baku had tripled ([JAMnews](https://jam-news.net/us-iran-conflict-boosts-azerbaijan-as-trans-caspian-demand-surges-fivefold/)). That route runs through Georgia: the Baku–Tbilisi–Kars railway and the ports of Poti and Batumi.

Two scenarios to keep in mind:

- **Hormuz stays closed or tolled.** Land routes and the Middle Corridor keep a lasting advantage. Logistics, warehousing and services for carriers in Georgia benefit.
- **A deal is reached, in Shenzhen or elsewhere.** Energy prices fall and sea transit wins back part of the traffic. The Middle Corridor stays useful as a backup route, but the urgency fades.

Either way, the right reflex is not to bet on one or the other, but to build projects that hold up in both.

## What I take from it as an investor or entrepreneur

1. **Watch where solutions are negotiated.** Today, that is Muscat, Doha, Islamabad and Shenzhen. Decisions that affect your energy and transport costs are made there.
2. **Measure your exposure to Hormuz.** Energy, sea freight, insurance: run the numbers with Brent at 100 dollars, then at 75.
3. **Watch three signals:** the outcome of the Shenzhen summit, the US answer to Iran's conditions, and transit volumes on the Middle Corridor.
