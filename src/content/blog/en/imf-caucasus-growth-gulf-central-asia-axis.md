On 4 November, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas announced that "everything is on the table" for new measures against Georgia, to be discussed in January ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/04/everything-is-on-the-table-eu-to-discuss-new-measures-against-georgia-in-january-kallas-sa)). Nine days later, the IMF presented its outlook for the Caucasus and Central Asia. The tone could not have been more different. Seen from Brussels, Georgia is drifting. Seen from the numbers, it is accelerating. And the IMF recommends opening the region even further towards the Gulf.

## What the IMF says

On 13 November, Roberto Cardarelli, deputy director of the IMF's Middle East and Central Asia Department, presented the regional outlook. His conclusion: growth remains solid in 2026, especially in oil-importing countries. Among the drivers, he cites the effects of the war in Ukraine: capital inflows, redirected trade, remittances and the arrival of skilled workers, "particularly in Armenia and Georgia" ([Trend](https://www.trend.az/casia/uzbekistan/4246018.html)).

The IMF's October figures speak for themselves ([Caucasus Watch](https://caucasuswatch.de/en/news/imf-projects-slower-growth-across-south-caucasus-in-2027-with-georgia-remaining-ahead.html)):

- **Georgia**: 7.2% growth in 2026, 5.7% in 2027.
- **Armenia**: 5.5% in both 2026 and 2027.
- **Azerbaijan**: 1.7% in 2026, 2.3% in 2027.

Over the first nine months of 2026, Geostat measures average growth of 7.4% in Georgia.

The IMF does not hide the shadows. Inflation exceeds the target in about six of the region's eight countries, because of energy ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/business/267348.html)). Its baseline scenario assumes a gradual normalisation of the Strait of Hormuz in 2027. If the crisis lasts, everyone will pay.

## The Gulf, a new natural partner

The same day, Koba Gvenetadze, the IMF's representative in Uzbekistan, discussed a recent Fund study on cooperation between the Caucasus, Central Asia and the Gulf Cooperation Council countries. According to him, it reflects the Gulf's growing interest in both regions. Its recommendations are very concrete: better logistics, better governance, fewer trade barriers, and digitised, harmonised customs procedures ([Trend](https://www.trend.az/casia/uzbekistan/4246049.html)).

This is not an abstract idea. According to Geostat, China was the largest foreign investor in Georgia in the second quarter of 2026, and the Gulf was in the top three ([Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/china-was-georgias-largest-foreign-investor-in-q2-2026-geostat/)). And the Gulf, for its part, is getting organised: on 10 November, the GCC countries decided to set up an advanced headquarters of their unified military command in Kuwait ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/10/gcc-agrees-to-set-up-advanced-military-command-headquarters-in-kuwait)). The Sultan of Oman, meanwhile, travelled to Beijing, a first since 1978 ([Gulf States Insights](https://gulfstatesinsights.substack.com/p/top-gulf-reads-15-november-2026)).

The Gulf asks no one for permission. It is diversifying its routes, its alliances and its investments. The Caucasus is on its list.

## Two readings of the same map

Europe reads Georgia through a political lens: elections, laws, alignment with Brussels. It is entitled to do so, and these questions matter. But that lens says nothing about flows.

The Caucasus, Central Asia and the Gulf read the same map through a lens of connections: trade routes, ports, pipelines, capital. In this reading, Georgia is an essential passage between the Caspian and the Black Sea, between Asia and Europe. The more dangerous the straits become, the more valuable the land routes.

I live in Tbilisi, and it is this second reading that I hear around me. Entrepreneurs in the region do not look at politics first. They look at an open economy, a simple tax system and a unique geographical position.

## What it means to invest in Georgia or start a business there

I am not saying Georgia is risk-free. Part of its growth relies on war-related flows, which can reverse. The European measures in January may weigh on certain sectors. You need to know this and keep an eye on it.

But three concrete points seem underestimated in Europe to me:

1. **Growth is measured, not promised.** The IMF, Geostat and the rating agencies are saying the same thing.
2. **Capital now comes from the East and the South**, not only from the West. That diversifies the country's sources of financing.
3. **The region is building a Gulf–Caucasus–Central Asia axis.** A company based in Tbilisi is well placed to benefit from it.

For a European entrepreneur, the question is therefore not to pick a side. It is to understand that the map is changing, and to take a position before everyone else does.
