On 5 November, TBC became the first bank in Georgia to offer payment by the palm of the hand. No card, no phone, no PIN: you place your hand on a reader and your account is debited. The announcement was picked up by [Trend.az](https://www.trend.az/scaucasus/georgia/4243278.html), [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/payments/news/tbc-georgia-launches-palm-payments-for-in-store-purchases) and several specialist outlets.

In Switzerland, we are still debating the end of cash. Here, in a country of 3.7 million people, a bank is launching a technology that Amazon has just abandoned in the United States. To me, this is not a gimmick. It is a good indicator of what **banking in Georgia** looks like today, and of what an entrepreneur can expect if they want to set up here.

So I wanted to go beyond the press release: how it works, what failed elsewhere, what we do not know yet, and what I take from it.

## How it works in practice

According to [TBC's official page](https://tbcbank.ge/en/other-products/palm-pay), the process is simple:

- **A one-time registration**, only at a physical terminal fitted with a palm reader. You scan your hand, then scan a QR code with the TBC app and choose the account to link.
- **One account per palm.** You can register both hands, each linked to a current account or a debit card account.
- **Clear limits:** a maximum of 1,500 lari per payment, in lari only. Each payment is confirmed by SMS.
- **Free of charge:** both registration and payments cost nothing.
- **Refunds:** you show the code received by SMS and place the same hand on the reader.

At launch, the service only works at partner merchants. The bank does not publish how many there are, but its page shows a few well-known Tbilisi brands, such as Entree or Under Armour. Sandro Rtveladze, Retail Business Director at TBC, sums up the ambition: bringing "the latest global financial technologies" to Georgia.

## Where Amazon gave up, others are moving ahead

What struck me was the timing. Amazon launched its Amazon One system in 2020 and installed it in more than 500 Whole Foods stores. It stopped working in retail on **3 June 2026**. The official reason fits in two words: "limited" customer adoption, according to [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-is-ending-its-palm-id-system-for-retail-amazon-one-as-it-closes-physical-stores/).

In Asia, the trend runs the other way. Tencent says its palm payment system already runs in around 1,500 7-Eleven stores in China and in the Beijing metro, according to [its own communication](https://palm.tencent.com/resources/amazon-one-discontinued-palm-payment-alternatives). In Europe, we are at the testing stage. Latvian start-up Handwave expanded its agreement with Visa in September for pilots in the Baltic states, with first roll-outs expected in 2026, according to [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/payments/news/handwave-expands-visa-agreement-for-biometric-payments).

The lesson applies to any business leader. A technology does not succeed because it is brilliant, but because customers adopt it. Amazon had the means, but not the usage. TBC has the advantage of a small market, where one bank reaches a large share of the population and can test quickly, measure and correct.

## What we do not know yet

I prefer to be clear: several questions remain open. Neither TBC's page nor the published articles say whether the technology reads the palm print or the vein pattern. They do not name the technology provider either, and they do not say where the biometric data is stored.

The legal framework exists. Georgia's [Law on Personal Data Protection](https://matsne.gov.ge/en/document/view/5827307), adopted on 14 June 2023, classifies biometric data used to identify a person as sensitive data. It requires whoever processes it to set out in writing the purpose, the retention period, and how the data is stored and destroyed. That is reassuring in principle. Now I am waiting for the bank to explain how it applies this in practice. Before registering my hand, that is the first question I will ask at the counter.

## Why it is a signal for entrepreneurs

This launch is part of an ecosystem that moves fast. TBC reports 7.2 million monthly digitally active customers across Georgia and Uzbekistan. Global Finance named it the most innovative bank in Central and Eastern Europe in April 2026. A few days before the launch, TBC also signed an agreement with American Express to widen acceptance of its cards in Georgia.

The public framework is keeping pace. The National Bank of Georgia is introducing an instant payment system meant to make transfers "faster" and cheaper, according to its governor Natia Turnava quoted by [Georgia Today](https://georgiatoday.ge/turnava-instant-payment-system-to-make-transfers-cheaper-faster/). And the sector is solid: its profits reached nearly 2.9 billion lari in the first nine months of 2026, according to [BM.ge](https://bm.ge/en/news/four-georgian-banks-report-losses-as-sector-profit-rises-to-gel-29-bln).

In practice, for anyone who wants to **open a bank account in Georgia** and run a company from here, this means three things:

1. **A bank that works well from abroad.** Full-featured apps, contactless payment almost everywhere, fast local transfers. You can run your company remotely without difficulty.
2. **A reflex to keep: compliance.** The more digital the bank, the more it checks. A clean file and real business activity remain the key, especially with the new European rules I explain in my article on the [CRD6 directive](/en/blog/crd6-11-january-2027-bank-account-georgia/).
3. **A mindset worth watching.** A bank founded in 1992 behaves like a start-up: it launches, measures and adjusts. Many Swiss SMEs could learn from that.

## My view

I do not know whether palm payment will become mainstream in Tbilisi. The market will decide, as it did for Amazon. But the message is clear: when it comes to banking services, Georgia is not catching up with Europe. On several points, it is ahead.
