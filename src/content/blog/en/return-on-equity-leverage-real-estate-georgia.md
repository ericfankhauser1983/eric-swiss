When people ask me "how much does it return?", I almost always answer with another question: return on what? On the price of the property, or on the money that actually left your pocket? The difference is huge. The first figure is the property's yield. The second is the **return on equity**. That is the one that measures your performance as an investor, and it changes as soon as a bank enters the equation.

Let's do the maths together, on a concrete case of **real estate investment in Georgia**, with and without a loan.

## Property yield versus return on equity

The property yield compares income with the total price. The return on equity compares the gain with your own contribution only. If you pay cash, the two are identical. If you borrow, they diverge, in one direction or the other.

That is the whole principle of leverage. The bank finances part of the property. Yet you receive 100% of the rent and 100% of the capital gain. In exchange, you pay it interest. If the property earns more than the debt costs, the difference boosts your return. If not, it destroys it.

The FFI blog published a good [method for calculating profitability](https://www.francefinance.ae/articles-blog/comment-calculer-la-rentabilite-dun-investissement-immobilier): gross, net of charges, then after tax, without forgetting the resale. I follow that logic and add the loan.

## My worked case in Tbilisi

The assumptions, all in dollars, since the market and loans work in that currency:

- **Total price, fees included: $100,000.** At $1,341 per m², the average price in Tbilisi in March 2026 according to [TBC Capital](https://tbccapital.ge/static/file/202604225003-tbilisi-residential-market-monthly-watch-march-2026-eng.pdf), that is about 70 m².
- **Gross rent: $8,100 a year**, or 8.1%, the average yield measured by TBC Capital.
- **Charges, vacancy and management: 20% of rent**, or $1,620.
- **Tax: 5% of gross rent**, or $405. This is Georgia's preferential rate on residential rental income, as [FFI](https://www.francefinance.ae/articles-blog/conference-paris-100-personnes-immobilier-georgie) points out.
- **Net rent: $6,075 a year**, or 6.1% of the price.
- **Appreciation: +4% a year.** That is the year-on-year rise measured in March 2026, and Galt & Taggart expects [single-digit growth](https://www.gbc.ge/en/news/Economics-news/galttaggart-predicts-single-digit-growth-in-apartment-prices-in-tbilisi-in-2026) in 2026.
- **Holding period: 5 years**, then resale. After two years of ownership, an individual pays no capital gains tax in Georgia. I leave out resale costs.

## Three scenarios, from cash to credit

**Scenario 1, cash.** You put in $100,000. You receive $6,075 net a year, or 6.1% on your equity. After five years, the property is worth about $121,700. Total gain: $30,400 in rent and $21,700 in capital gain, or $52,000.

**Scenario 2, dollar loan from a Georgian bank.** Banks generally ask foreigners for a 30 to 40% down payment. For a dollar loan, TBC quoted a rate of 7.5% in the first quarter of 2025, and an effective rate of 9.4% including fees and insurance, according to [PB Services](https://pbservices.ge/blog/getting-a-mortgage-in-georgia/). I assume 40% down and $60,000 borrowed at 9% over 10 years. The monthly payment is $760, or $9,120 a year.

**Scenario 3, cheaper credit.** This is a textbook assumption: the same structure, but with debt at 5% over 20 years. The monthly payment falls to $396.

| | Cash | Loan at 9% | Loan at 5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial equity | $100,000 | $40,000 | $40,000 |
| Interest paid in year 1 | 0 | $5,240 | $2,960 |
| Rental return on equity, year 1 | 6.1% | 2.1% | 7.8% |
| Annual cash flow after loan payment | +$6,075 | −$3,045 | +$1,325 |
| Net gain over 5 years, resale included | $52,000 | $29,800 | $38,200 |
| Average annual return on equity (IRR) | 9.6% | 10.6% | 15.0% |

What I take from this table comes down to three points.

**On rent alone, the 9% loan is poor leverage.** The property earns 6.1% net, the debt costs 9%. The rental return on equity falls to 2.1%. And every year, you have to put in around $3,000 of your own money to cover the payment.

**It is the capital gain that rescues the deal.** With a 4% rise per year, the overall return on equity goes from 9.6% to 10.6%. But the gap is thin, and you have had to add about $15,000 over five years.

**When debt costs less than the property's net yield, leverage really works.** At 5%, the rent covers the payment, and the return on equity rises to 15% a year.

## Leverage works the other way too

People often forget that leverage amplifies both sides. I re-ran scenario 2 with two other appreciation assumptions:

- **Flat prices for five years:** 6.1% a year in cash, but only 3.3% with the 9% loan.
- **Prices rising 8% a year:** 13.3% in cash, 17.3% with the loan.

Leverage does not create return. It multiplies the outcome of the bet you are making on the market. In Tbilisi, prices have risen strongly in recent years, but the pace is slowing: +20% in 2023, +11.6% in 2024 and +4% in 2025 for new builds, according to Galt & Taggart. I never build a financing plan that only works if prices go up.

## My rules before borrowing

1. **Compare the real cost of debt, fees included, with the property's net yield.** Not with the gross yield printed in the brochure.
2. **Check the cash flow year by year.** If the rent does not cover the payment, set aside the reserve from day one.
3. **Borrow in the currency of your rental income, or know your currency risk.** A loan in francs backed by rent in dollars adds a bet on exchange rates.
4. **Test the no-growth scenario.** If it is unacceptable, the structure is too tight.

Credit is neither good nor bad in itself. It is a tool to be calculated, case by case, according to your situation and your horizon.
