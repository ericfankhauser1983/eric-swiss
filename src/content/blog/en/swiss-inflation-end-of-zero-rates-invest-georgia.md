For years, money cost almost nothing in Switzerland. That era may be coming to an end. In October, inflation jumped to 1.5% year on year, and several economists are now considering a rise in the SNB's policy rate. For anyone wondering whether to start **investing in Georgia from Switzerland**, the question is no longer just about yield. It is also about the cost of the money you leave idle, or borrow, at home.

## Inflation that surprised everyone

The figures from the Federal Statistical Office, published on 3 November, caught economists off guard. The consumer price index rose **1.5% year on year** in October, against 1% in September and 0.4% in July. It is the highest level since December 2023. The consensus expected between 1.1% and 1.4% ([swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/various/inflation-soars-to-1-5-per-cent-in-october/92318356)).

The driver is clear: energy. Petroleum products cost about 40% more than a year earlier, a direct consequence of the crisis in the Gulf. Core inflation, excluding energy and fresh products, remains at 0.5%. So we are not in a price spiral. But we are no longer in the world of "zero forever" either.

SNB chairman Martin Schlegel acknowledged that inflationary pressures had increased. According to him, the bank is ready to adjust its policy if price stability is threatened, even though no second-round effects are visible yet ([StockWire X](https://stockwirex.com/news/snb-inflation-schlegel-swiss-cpi-november-2026/)).

## Will the SNB raise rates?

The policy rate is at 0%. The next decision comes on **10 December**. At the end of September, the SNB saw inflation at only 0.7% in 2026 and 0.8% in 2027. Those forecasts are already outdated.

According to FFI, which reports the views of the financial community, EFG believes the probability of a hike in December or March has increased, and Raiffeisen does not rule out the SNB ending its wait-and-see stance as early as December. UBS sees no urgency, but acknowledges that the risks are rising. Markets expect a first hike in early 2027.

I am not making a forecast on the date. I simply note that the direction of travel has changed.

## What it changes for a Swiss homeowner

Today, mortgage rates remain low. On 7 November, FFI noted about 0.91% for a SARON mortgage, 1.45% for a 5-year fixed rate and 1.56% for 10 years. But a SARON mortgage tracks the SNB rate directly: at the first quarter-point hike, the monthly payment goes up.

Three other points deserve attention:

- **The reference mortgage rate**, at 1.25%, will be published again on 1 December. Each quarter-point increase allows landlords to raise rents by about 3%. Tenants will feel it too.
- **Swiss property prices** keep rising, by about 3% in 2026 according to UBS, with marked imbalances around Lake Geneva. Buying expensive with credit that is getting dearer means a yield that melts away.
- **The abolition of the imputed rental value**, approved in September 2025 by 57.7%, will take effect on 1 January 2029. It comes with the end of the mortgage interest deduction. For an indebted homeowner, the tax calculation changes completely.

In short: in Switzerland, real estate remains a safe haven, but it yields little. Gross yields are low, and more expensive credit erodes them further.

## Investing in Georgia from Switzerland: comparing like with like

I am not saying you should sell your flat in Lausanne to buy in Batumi. I am saying you should look at where your savings are working.

In Georgia, rental income is taxed at 5%, and a flat in Tbilisi shows a gross rental yield of around 8.6% according to Galt & Taggart, as I explained in [my article on Swiss salaries](/en/blog/swiss-salaries-2027-put-your-savings-to-work/). Credit there is more expensive than in Switzerland: TBC Bank advertises mortgages from 6.3%. That is precisely why I never make it the starting point of the calculation. Instead, I use developers' interest-free payment plans during construction, then credit on a case-by-case basis at handover.

The logic is simple, and I detailed it in my article on [return on equity](/en/blog/return-on-equity-leverage-real-estate-georgia/): what matters is what the money you actually put in earns, compared with what it costs you.

For a Swiss saver, this means:

1. **Keep in Switzerland what should stay there**: your main residence and your safety reserve.
2. **Stop leaving the rest idle** at a real rate that turns negative again as soon as inflation exceeds the interest on the account.
3. **Diversify part of your wealth** towards a market where rent comfortably covers the cost of money.

## What I take from it

The end of zero rates is not a catastrophe. It is a return to normal. But it ends an illusion: that you only had to buy in Switzerland with free money to get rich. When money has a price again, you have to put it where it earns more than it costs.
