This week, UBS published its annual salary survey. The verdict is clear: in 2027, Swiss companies plan to raise salaries by **1% on average**. Once inflation is deducted, the real gain falls to **0.2%**. I ran an SME in Switzerland for more than ten years. I know what a pay-rise budget represents, and I also know what it cannot do: make you wealthy.

I do not see this as bad news. I see it as a signal. In Switzerland today, it is no longer your salary that builds wealth. It is the capital you put to work.

## What the figures really say

The [UBS survey](https://www.ubs.com/global/de/media/display-page-ndp/de-20261103-ubs-outlook-2027.html), conducted among nearly 400 companies, forecasts a nominal increase of 1.0% in 2027, after 1.1% in 2026. Expected inflation is 0.8%. Real wages therefore grow by only 0.2%, compared with 0.4% expected for 2026.

The gaps between sectors remain small. Energy leads with 1.5%, watchmaking brings up the rear with 0.7%. According to [20 minutes](https://www.20min.ch/fr/story/suisse-ubs-predit-une-augmentation-des-salaires-de-1-en-2027-103661191), three quarters of companies do not pay more for artificial intelligence skills. And 40% of them expect unemployment to rise in 2027.

Meanwhile, health insurance premiums rise by **5% on average** in 2027. The average premium reaches 412 francs a month, according to Federal Office of Public Health figures reported by [Compareprime](https://compareprime.ch/blog/prime-assurance-maladie-2027-suisse). For many households, this increase alone swallows the pay rise.

## The maths for an average household

Take an annual salary of 100,000 francs. A 1% rise represents 1,000 francs gross a year. After social charges and tax, much less remains.

For a couple, the premium increase costs around 20 francs a month per adult, or nearly 500 francs a year. Add inflation on the rest of your spending, and the 2027 rise almost entirely disappears.

And savings? The average savings account rate in Switzerland is **0.11%** a year, according to [moneyland.ch](https://www.moneyland.ch/fr/taux-interet-comptes-epargne-suisse-comparaison-2026). On 50,000 francs, that earns 55 francs a year. With 0.8% inflation, those 50,000 francs lose around 400 francs of purchasing power every year. The Swiss National Bank kept its policy rate at 0% in September 2026, as [Neo Hypothèque](https://www.neo-hypotheque.ch/blog/bns-septembre-2026-taux-hypothecaires/) notes. Nothing points to a quick change.

In other words, money sleeping in Switzerland is not really sleeping: it is slowly shrinking.

## Salary pays for life, capital builds wealth

That is the lesson I learned as an entrepreneur. A salary, even a comfortable one, is first of all for living. Wealth is built elsewhere: in what the money you have already set aside earns.

The good news is that you do not need to be rich to start. Above all, you need to understand one thing: what matters is **the return on your money**, not just the return of the property you buy. I explained this principle in my article on [return on equity](/en/blog/return-on-equity-leverage-real-estate-georgia/).

Take the same 50,000 francs. Placed in an apartment in Tbilisi, with a gross rental yield of 8.6% according to [Galt & Taggart](https://www.gbc.ge/en/news/Economics-news/galttaggart-predicts-single-digit-growth-in-apartment-prices-in-tbilisi-in-2026) and rent taxed at only 5%, they produce around 4,000 francs of net rent a year. That is the equivalent of four 2027 pay rises. And that is before even counting the property's appreciation or the leverage of a loan, which can double the return on your stake.
