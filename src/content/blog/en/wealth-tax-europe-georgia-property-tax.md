For twenty years, Europe abolished its wealth taxes one after another. This autumn, the tide is turning. Hungary is creating one, the Netherlands is tightening the taxation of assets, and the European Parliament is studying a minimum tax on large fortunes. For an investor, the conclusion is simple: moving from one EU country to another protects you less and less. That is why I see **property tax in Georgia** not as a bonus, but as part of the return.

## What changed in Europe in a few weeks

On 6 October, Hungary's new Prime Minister, Péter Magyar, announced a wealth tax from 1 January 2027. The rate is **1% a year above one billion forints**, about 3 million dollars, and 1.5% above 100 billion. Real estate, investments, company shares and foreign assets are included, and debts are deductible ([EY Hungary](https://www.ey.com/en_hu/insights/tax/hungary-new-government-plans-introduction-of-wealth-tax-for-individuals)). Parliament still has to pass the bill.

In the Netherlands, the "box 3" reform presented at the end of September plans to tax gains actually realised on most assets from 2028, and on all of them from 2030. The reference rate is 36%.

In Brussels, a hearing of the European Parliament's tax subcommittee on 12 October examined the EU Tax Observatory's proposal: a **minimum tax of 2% on fortunes above 100 million euros**, with no exemption by asset type. It is only a report in preparation, with no obligation for member states. But the subject is now on the table at European level.

Meanwhile, Spain has extended its solidarity tax on large fortunes, created in 2023 as a "temporary" measure. In France, the real-estate wealth tax still applies above 1.3 million euros, and the "Zucman tax" was rejected by the National Assembly on 16 October. The debate, however, is not over.

## What Switzerland and Norway teach us

I am Swiss, so I know the subject well. At home, the wealth tax is cantonal and has always existed, at a few per mille depending on the canton. It remains bearable because it is low and income tax stays moderate. On 30 November 2025, Swiss voters rejected by 78% an initiative that wanted to tax inheritances above 50 million francs at 50%.

Norway tried the opposite. After it raised its wealth tax in 2022, dozens of the country's wealthiest taxpayers left, and many of them moved… to Switzerland ([The Local Norway](https://www.thelocal.no/20251023/how-norways-wealth-tax-drove-out-the-rich-without-breaking-the-bank)). A wealth tax does not drive everyone away. But it drives away those who have a choice.

## The calculation few owners make

A wealth tax is not paid with wealth. It is paid with the income that wealth produces. That is where European rental property becomes fragile.

Take an apartment that yields **2% net** a year, which is common in Paris, Geneva or Amsterdam. A 1% wealth tax absorbs **half** of that income. At 4% net, it still takes a quarter. And that calculation ignores the income tax on the rent itself.

This is the point I explain most often: investors look at the gross yield, rarely at what is left after every layer of tax. Yet what is left is what builds wealth.

## Property tax in Georgia, without embellishment

Georgia has **no wealth tax**. The main rules for an individual investing in an apartment are as follows:

- **5% on residential rent**, on the gross amount, if no expenses are deducted ([PwC Tax Summaries](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/georgia/individual/income-determination));
- **no capital gains tax** when you sell a home held for more than two years; before two years, the gain is taxed at 5%;
- **a limited property tax**: since the Tax Code reform passed at the end of September 2026, it only applies to households whose family income exceeds **100,000 lari** a year, about 37,000 dollars. The threshold used to be 40,000 lari. Above it, the rate is 0.8% to 1% of market value, depending on the municipality ([RegFollower](https://regfollower.com/georgia-increases-property-tax-exemption-threshold-to-gel-100000/)).

I want to be precise on this last point, because you often read that "there is no property tax in Georgia". That is not quite accurate. A high-income investor can be liable, and how household income is calculated depends on your residence situation. I have it checked by a tax adviser for every client.

Let's take a concrete example. An apartment in Tbilisi bought for 150,000 dollars and let for 12,000 dollars a year, an 8% gross yield. The tax on rent costs 600 dollars. If the household is above the income threshold, property tax at 0.8% adds 1,200 dollars. In total, tax takes about **15% of the rent** in the worst case, and 5% in the usual case. And it only applies to the Georgian property, not to your whole estate.

## Diversifying outside Europe, yes, but with method

I am not saying Europe will tax everyone tomorrow. The Hungarian and European projects target very large fortunes first. But tax history shows one thing: a tax created for a few often ends up widening. The threshold falls, the rate rises, and the "temporary" exception becomes permanent, as in Spain.

Diversifying part of your property portfolio outside the EU, in a country where the rules are simple and stable, is a form of insurance. It is not about escaping tax, but about not putting all your assets under the same tax roof. And Georgia has another quality I appreciate: its tax rules are short and readable, and when they change, as they did this autumn, it tends to be in the taxpayer's favour.
